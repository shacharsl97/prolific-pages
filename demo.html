Six practice examples

These six examples are practice: you answer, submit, and are then shown a worked solution — the sequence of questions that reaches it, and several mistakes that are easy to make. Nothing here is sent to us and nothing here is scored by us — this practice is for you. Work as you would on real data, because the rounds that follow it have no worked solutions.

Each marked statement gets one card. It opens with the one question that can end the card early — whether the statement is hallucinated at all — and then asks for the operation and the final question that both the source and the hallucinated statement answer. What it asks for next depends on the operation: a replace needs R, the value you replace the hallucination with, and the source attribution it is copied from; a deletion needs the explanation of which questions were not answerable, and why deletion was chosen. The revision is written once for the whole summary, with every span correction applied; it is filled in for you as you answer, and you can edit it. Grammar and fluency are applied after the factual revision, in their own box.

Five of these six have one marked statement; the sixth has two that must be repaired. In the production annotation a summary can carry up to four. Three of the six are harder than they look, and on one of them the right answer is to change nothing.

Do not use ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or any other language model, here or in the production batch. We are studying how people write faithful minimal revisions, so we need your own answers.
The revision procedure
  1. Find a question that both the marked statement and the source answer — the marked statement answers it falsely, the source answers it faithfully.
  2. Narrow it: ask a more and more specific question while the source can still answer, until you reach the most specific question both of them answer.
  3. The question has to be one the source can actually answer. Do not keep a presupposition the source does not support, and do not add a specific value just to match the original's specificity.
  4. Replace the unfaithful part with the source's answer to that question, leaving the rest of the sentence intact. Where the source supports more than one answer, take the one closest in specificity to the value the marked statement claimed — neither vaguer nor more detailed than what it attempted. Where the source has wording that fits the slot, use the source's wording verbatim rather than paraphrasing.
  5. If no meaningful question exists, delete the unfaithful part. Rule of thumb: if the answer you found could be dropped from the revision with no loss of information, it is vacuous — delete instead of inserting it.
  6. Then make the grammar or lexical adjustment that renders the text coherent, without adding any new information. That goes in the grammar box, after the revision.
  7. Before you submit, check the revision against the marked statement: is there anything faithful the marked statement implied that the revision has lost? Keep all faithful information the marked statement entailed.
  8. If the marked statement is faithful, tick This isn't hallucinated and say what supports it. If only part of it is unfaithful, copy the true hallucinated statement into Span adjustment and revise only that part.
