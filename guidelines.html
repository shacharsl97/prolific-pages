Minimal revisionannotation guidelines

1Terminology

Target text: The text we scan for hallucinations. Could be LLM generated or human written. We attempt to find and correct all non factual details (with respect to the source text) within this text.

Source: The reference text that the target text is intended to reflect. It serves as our ground truth and provides the factual evidence against which the target text is compared to detect hallucinated statements.

Statement: The different propositions or units of information the target text consists of. Each statement may be entailed or contradicted by the source, or neither (“neutral”). A statement is a hallucination to revise when it is contradicted or merely neutral (i.e. not entailed neither contradicted) — both are non-faithful; only entailed statements are left untouched.

  • For example, if the target text is “Danny ate a green apple”, it can be decomposed into two different statements: “Danny ate an apple” and “The apple was green”.

2Revision guidelines

You are provided with a ground-truth source, a hallucination-containing target text and a set of marked hallucinated statements within that target text. Your job is to revise the target text and correct all hallucinations in the optimal and most minimal manner, according to the following criteria:

GOAL — Fix the marked non-faithful statements in a summary so that everything it says is supported by its source, while changing the meaning as little as possible.

  • A minimal revision should aim to preserve as much as possible the target text’s author's original information and intent while editing its content to be faithful to the source text.
  • Faithfulness is judged under reasonable world knowledge and high-likelihood inference, but you may not introduce non-trivial facts absent from the source, even if they're true in the real world.
    • Common knowledge: Argentina is in South America.
    • Non common-knowledge: Lionel Messi was born in Rosario.
    • What counts as common knowledge is defined in the Common knowledge section below, where you will first need it.
  • The intent we aim to keep is the informational intent (defined below) of the original text, that is, the information the author tried to give when they wrote the target text.

Please note that while mostly correct, certain detected hallucinated statements might be non-accurately marked; so if you feel like the marked hallucination is faithful to the source text - or that only part of it is hallucinated - please mark that and continue to the next hallucination. In addition, do revise only the part of the originally marked span you believe is actually not-faithful to the source text.

Informational intent

A good revision is judged on two things, and the procedure in the sections that follow exists to satisfy both:

  1. Minimal information loss. Every source-faithful fact the hallucinated statement entails should also survive the revision. If both the hallucination and the source imply some fact, the revision must still imply it.
  2. Minimal specificity change. Answer in the same facet the author was writing about, at as close a level of detail as the source allows.

Both are illustrated by the worked examples that follow: the first shows information loss, the second shows specificity change.

To satisfy both at once, we search for the most specific question that can be answered by both the source and the hallucination (that is, the hallucination answers it falsely, and the source answers it faithfully). The best revision is then the faithful answer to that question, based on the source text.

This is the information the author (assume a human author here) intended to provide. This intent may be captured by phrasing a hypothetical question that the author intended to answer. In a hallucinated statement, the author provided an answer not faithful to the source text.

When constructing these questions, make your best assessment at understanding the author's intent and try to construct a question that the author actually intended to answer (or give information about) when they wrote their original hallucinated statement.

A good revision preserves all salvageable faithful information from the hallucination — that is, whatever the hallucinated statement happened to get right — and answers a question as close as possible to the original question the author intended to answer - with faithful information from the source.

A properly revised text communicates the same informational intent of the original hallucination, but with faithful source-backed information.

Consider the following example:

  • Source: “John Lennon was born in Liverpool”

Target: “Lennon was born in Germany”

The best revision for this example pair is “Lennon was born in England”:

  • It preserves the salvageable faithful statement “Lennon was born in Europe” which is faithful to the source and implied by the target text.
  • It answers the question “in which country was Lennon born?” which is the exact question the target attempted to answer.
  • It is entailed by the source text. (Liverpool being in England is considered common knowledge)

Answerability

Throughout, we say a text answers a question Q (with respect to a source text S) if it entails a direct answer to Q. That means that the text may provide an answer that is more specific (detailed) than Q strictly requires, but the answer expected by Q can still be implied from it. For example:

  • "John Lennon was born in Liverpool" answers "in which country was John Lennon born?", because being born in Liverpool entails being born in England.
  • When we say the hallucination answers Q, we mean only that it supplies an answer of the type Q asks for — while that answer will, clearly, be false (hallucinated) relative to the source.
  • The answers to both questions should be complete statements so their specificity can be compared.
    • E.g: The answer should be "Lennon was born in England" and not just "England".

Common knowledge

Common knowledge is defined by what the text assumes that “typical” readers of the text would know.

  • For example, in a medical journal, the knowledge of common medical equipment and processes may be expected.

A practical test: if you would have to look it up, it is not common knowledge. The definition above is about the text's assumed reader rather than about you personally, but when you are unsure, this is an easier question to ask yourself.

The line is not always sharp. Consider four cases, in order:

  • The source says the match was played in Munich; the target says it was played in Germany. Munich is a city in Germany - Common knowledge — no reader of this text would need to look that up.
  • The source says the summit opened on a Tuesday; the target says it opened on a weekday. Common knowledge — it follows from the source by ordinary reasoning.
  • The source refers to “the magazine's Film Awards ceremony”; the target calls it “the magazine's annual Film Awards ceremony”. Genuinely arguable. A regular reader of that magazine may well know the ceremony is annual; another reader would have to check. Two careful annotators can disagree here.
  • The source never gives a celebrity's age; the target calls her “the 62-year-old actress”. Not common knowledge — even if it is true, it is a fact you would have to look up, so it is an introduced fact and must be revised.

Where exactly the line falls will differ a little between annotators, and that is expected — it is part of what makes the task hard. Use your discretion and make the best call.

Specificity

We define specificity of question as follows:

  • A question Q1 is more specific than Q2, with respect to a given text, if every answer to Q1 that is faithful to the text also answers Q2, while some answer to Q2 faithful to the text does not answer Q1.
  • A question is most specific with respect to a text if there’s no other question that is more specific than it with respect to that text.
  • A question is most specific with respect to both the source and the target texts if there’s no other question that is more specific with respect to both.
  • Reminder: as noted in the definition of “answerable” above, a question would be regarded as answerable by the source, or the target, only if it is clearly implied from the respective text by common knowledge expected from a reader of that text.

Applying the test to “Lennon was born in Germany”: the question “Where was Lennon born?” is answerable by both, but the source still allows many answers (Liverpool, and the implied England, the UK, Europe), while the hallucination answers Germany. Narrowing to “In which country was Lennon born?” leaves the source only one answer — England — which is the best revision for this example.

When helpful, we recommend an iterative question generation process, where an annotator starts with a less specific question (Q1), inspects the derived possible answers from the source texts, tests whether a more specific question can be asked, until it has reached the most specific granularity (Qmax).

Note: The question must be one the source can actually answer. When narrowing, make sure the question you settle on is answerable by the source — do not keep a presupposition the source does not support, or add a specific value just to match the original text’s specificity.

Additional example:

  • Source: “The horses got stuck in mud as they were being ridden on a beach by the slipway near Quail Holme Road, Knott End at about 10:30 BST on Sunday. The Fleetwood-Knott End ferry and two kayakers helped with the rescue operation…”
  • Target: Two horses have been rescued from the River Ribble in Lancashire.
  • Revision A (incorrect): Two horses have been rescued from the River Ribble mud in Lancashire.
  • Revision B (correct): Two horses have been rescued from the River Ribble a beach near Knott End in Lancashire

The target said the horses were rescued from “the River Ribble”. The informational intent here was to provide the geographical location (from where were the horses rescued?).

However, the specific question “from which river were the horses rescued?” is not answerable by the source. The answerable question is the coarser “from which place were they rescued?”, whose faithful complete sentence answer is “the horses were rescued from a beach near Knott End”.

  • “The horses were rescued from the mud” is not a good revision as it states which substance the horses were stuck in, and misses the geographic locational intent.
  • We would replace “the river Ribble” with “a beach near Knott End” - the span from the complete sentence answer that can replace the hallucinated span cleanly.

Notice that the deliverable is the revision R, not the question. If two different questions lead to the same R, both are fine. (However, we still ask you to provide the question in your annotation to understand the reason for the revision chosen).

Deletion

If we can not find a meaningful question answerable by both the target and the source, there is no valid intent-preserving replacement for the hallucinated content.

In this case - we delete the hallucinated clause completely, while, if needed, making sure to revise the target phrasing to keep it grammatical and fluent.

  • However, make sure to avoid adding any new information from the source text not present in the original target text. Constrain yourself to grammatical and phrasing adjustments.
  • Example: the target says “The 50-year-old blond man quit his job”, but the source never mentions his hair colour, and offers no other description of his appearance that could take its place. There is no question about his appearance that both texts can answer, so delete the word: “The 50-year-old man quit his job”.

3Annotation Workflow

You will be provided with the source text, and a target text with different marked hallucinations (already detected)

Preliminaries

  1. As noted above, the marked spans may not be perfectly aligned, in which case we should adjust the span to be minimal and only later revise.
    1. A span is “minimal” if removing any word from it leaves a faithful statement.
  2. A span may contain several hallucinated details, in which case we should split the span into each mentioned detail and only later revise
    1. E.g: the marked span “green apple” in the text “Danny ate a green apple” should be split into two spans “green” and “apple”, as each of the two are distinct pieces of information that may be revised or replaced in a different way.

For each single marked hallucinated statement (span) and texts you will:

  1. Adjust the marked span and convert it into a complete statement H.
    1. That is, converting “England” to “Lennon was born in England”
  2. Attempt to find Q1, a specific question answerable from both H and the source text.
    1. The answer to the question should be exactly H, in complete.
    2. If Q1 was found, inspect its specificity and the candidate source answers and if needed, continue to find more and more specific questions, until finding Qmax, the most specific question answerable by both the target and the source.
    3. Replace the marked span with the part of R that answers Qmax, leaving the rest of the sentence intact. You will type Qmax into the question field in the tool.
  3. If no meaningful Q exists, remove the span related to H from the target completely.
  • Rule of thumb: if you can eventually notice that the answer (to Qmax) that you found could be dropped from the revised target without any loss of information, then it's vacuous — so just delete the span instead of inserting anything.
    • E.g. if the source says nothing about where Lennon was born, don't revise "Lennon was born in Germany" to "Lennon was born in a country"; just delete.
  1. After replacing/deleting, make the necessary grammar or lexical adjustment to render the text coherent and sensible — in places where your own edits broke it — but without adding any new information beyond the answer to Qmax

After revising, validate that you positively answer these questions:

  • Is this replacement the same type and same specificity level of H?
  • Did the author really intend to answer the chosen question when writing the target text?

Additional points

  • Before submitting, check the revised text against the hallucination again: did you lose any salvageable faithful information? We aim to keep all faithful information that was entailed by the hallucinated text. In such a case, look for a question whose answer will preserve this faithful information from the target, if one exists.
  • Where the source provides wording that fits the slot, use the source’s wording verbatim rather than paraphrasing.
  • Make the smallest edit that restores faithfulness and preserves informational goal - do not improve fluency, style or content of the original non-hallucinated text
    • However, when you do need to phrase a statement yourself, try to sound as natural as possible.
  • Please do not use any LLMs, we are attempting to examine natural human writing.

After finishing each source-target texts pair, we will provide you with several meta-metric questions useful for our research. Please take your time to answer them properly.
